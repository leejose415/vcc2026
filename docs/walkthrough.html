The open problem

mse has been 0 on every submission. Our own generator, fed the true effects, scores mse 0.77, so the cells are not the problem; our predicted effects are (diagnosis).

Nine questions, short answers

  1. What does "turning effects into cells" mean? The model's output is a table of numbers: for every knockdown × gene, a predicted log2 change in mean expression. The challenge wants cells: integer count matrices, 400 cells per knockdown. Two steps bridge the gap: finishing decides which genes change and by how much, and the generator edits real control cells' counts to realise those changes. Steps 6–7.
  2. How are the top 2,000 genes chosen, and are they raised arbitrarily? Per knockdown, rank every gene expressed above 5 CPM in the line's controls by |Δ| / SE (how convincing Model 2's prediction is) and keep the top 2,000; every other gene is set to no change. The raise is not arbitrary in size: each kept gene is lifted to the smallest change that a 400-cell group would show at z = 5 against that line's own controls, computed per gene from its control mean and variance. It is arbitrary with respect to biology: a predicted change smaller than that floor is overridden. Step 6.
  3. What is the detection floor? For each gene, the smallest up- and down-change that would be statistically visible (z = 5) in 400 cells against the line's controls. Noisy genes need bigger changes. Without it, only 48–72% of the genes we called came out significant in our own generated cells; with it, 92–94%. Worked numbers.
  4. How reliable is H1 as a benchmark? Reliable for big decisions and for the number of genes, unreliable for score sizes, and wrong once on a calling-scheme change (v4). Keep it as a screen, add a second validation line, and use leaderboard submissions to settle changes that trade one metric for another. Track record.
  5. Were 31 PCA programs identified? 31 components rise above the measurement-noise edge in the training data, but we never used 31: the models used k = 5 (corrected) and k = 20 (programs only), from a tested grid of 5 / 7 / 10 / 20. PCA layer.
  6. What is k80, and what does 7 mean? Not "80% of the noise": k80 is the smallest k whose components hold 80% of the variance above the noise. On our fits it is 7 (7 components hold 81.5% of the above-noise variance). How it is computed.
  7. What is the "shared response"? The part of a knockdown's effect that is the same whatever gene is knocked down in that line (stress, slowed growth, CRISPR machinery, the screen's culture conditions). It is measured as the average change over all knockdowns of a line, and it is exactly what the leaderboard's 0 already knows. Details.
  8. A worked pds example? From 400 cells × 18,533 genes per knockdown down to one rank. Worked example.
  9. A worked mse example, and what is "5%"? On H1, predicting each line's true average response leaves 95.3% of the real squared movement unexplained, so it explains about 5%; that is the bar for any mse above 0. Worked example.

The task

For each challenge cell line (A, B, C now; D, E, F for the final ranking) and each of its 300 knockdowns, submit 400 simulated cells with counts for 18,533 genes. For the challenge lines we have only unperturbed control cells (about 18,400 per line), no knockdown data at all. Everything we know about what a knockdown does comes from other cell lines' screens, so the problem is transfer: predict a known knockdown's effect in a line we have never seen perturbed.

We validate on H1 (human embryonic stem cells from the same lab, Arc, as A–F; the 2025 challenge line): hold H1 out of the model, predict 75 of its knockdowns, generate cells from H1's controls, and score them with the official evaluator against H1's real knockdown cells.

The pipeline, step by step

  1. Collect knockdown screens from other lines

    HCT116 and HEK293T (Orion; stored as per-knockdown sums), K562 and RPE1 (Replogle; single cells), HepG2 and Jurkat (Nadig; single cells), CD4 T cells stimulated 48 h (Marson; effect table only) and H1 (Arc; single cells). One "vote" per background: the three T-cell tables count as one.

  2. Clean: keep only knockdowns that worked

    Per knockdown and dataset (scripts/knockdown_filters.py): at least 100 cells; the target gene visibly expressed in that dataset's controls (≥ 5 CPM or ≥ 0.1 UMI per cell, by rule); and the knockdown worked: the target dropped by at least half (log2 fold change ≤ −1) and significantly (Benjamini–Hochberg adjusted p < 0.05). Failures are dropped, never filled.

  3. Build effect tables

    One table per source (scripts/build_panel_sources.py): rows are knockdowns (our 300, then H1's), columns are the 18,533 genes. Each entry is the gene's log2 fold change against that dataset's own controls, log2((mean CPM + 0.5) / (control mean CPM + 0.5)), with its standard error. Not measured (NaN) is kept distinct from no change (0); genes below 1 CPM in a source's controls are NaN there.

  4. Model 2: average the lines (random-effects meta-analysis)

    For each knockdown × gene, average the backgrounds that measured it, each weighted by 1 / (SE² + τ²). τ² is a per-gene "how much do lines genuinely disagree" term, estimated by DerSimonian–Laird over all knockdowns measured in ≥ 2 lines, leaving out the knocked-down gene itself. Output: a predicted change Δ and its standard error per knockdown × gene. The knocked-down gene's own drop is the mean of the sources' measured on-target drops. Validation fits leave H1 out; the final fit uses all eight backgrounds including H1.

    Nothing about A, B or C enters, so all three get the same Δ table. Coverage of the 300 challenge knockdowns: 18 have no source line (only their own drop is predicted), 56 have one, 96 two, 72 three, 53 four, 5 five.

  5. Optional: PCA + ridge correction

    Built and validated in vcc_2026_3, not used in any submission. See the PCA layer.

  6. Finishing: which genes change, by how much

    This is where submissions have differed most. In the best submission (v3):

    • Gate: only genes above 5 CPM in that line's own controls can change (the scorer ignores the rest).
    • Top-N call: per knockdown, rank those genes by |Δ| / SE and keep the top 2,000; every other gene is set to no change.
    • Detection floor: each kept gene's change becomes sign(Δ) × max(|Δ|, floorgene), so that the generated cells actually show it.
    • Limits: changes capped at ±8 log2; the knocked-down gene's own drop applied separately (its remaining fraction clipped to 1–90%).

    Why calling at all: the real data have about 1,930 significantly changed genes per H1 knockdown. v2's significance gate kept only about 29, which cost us on every DE metric; top 2,000 took the leaderboard from −0.054 to +0.091. v4 tried no calling at all (every gene at 0.75 × Δ): better on H1, worse on the leaderboard (fidelity fell from −0.09 to −0.24).

    The detection floor in numbers

    For gene g with per-cell control mean m and variance v, a change by factor f in 400 cells is visible at

    z = m·|f − 1| / √( v·f²/400 + v/n_controls ) ≥ 5

    With many control cells this depends only on the gene's cell-to-cell coefficient of variation (CV). Smallest visible changes:

    Gene's CV across cellssmallest visible increasesmallest visible decrease
    0.6 (well expressed, steady)×1.18 (+0.23 log2)×0.87 (−0.20 log2)
    1.2 (about 1 count per cell)×1.43 (+0.51 log2)×0.77 (−0.38 log2)
    2.5 (sparse, bursty)×2.67 (+1.42 log2)×0.62 (−0.70 log2)

    On H1, the floor raised the median called change from 0.18 to 0.29 log2. z = 5 was chosen by checking our own generated cells: at z = 4 only 80% of called genes came out significant, at z = 5, 92–94%.

  7. Generate cells

    For each knockdown in line A (generate_one in vcc3_generate.py):

    • Start from real cells: take 400 real control cells of line A. The same 400 are reused for every knockdown, so differences between knockdowns come from Δ, not from which cells were drawn.
    • Keep composition consistent: convert the Δs into per-gene multiplicative factors, renormalised so each cell's composition still sums to one.
    • Decrease: randomly thin that cell's counts (binomial thinning), which keeps each cell's own state (cycle, depth).
    • Increase: add counts drawn with the gene's own overdispersion, estimated from line A's controls.
    • Library sizes and cell counts follow the line's metadata.

    Fed H1's true effects, this generator scores 0.85 overall (mse 0.77), so it reproduces real cells well.

  8. Validate, select, submit

    Score each candidate on 75 H1 knockdowns with the official evaluator (cell-eval2) and the leaderboard's own scaling; choose on the scaled overall; a paired bootstrap over the 75 knockdowns separates real wins (beyond about ±0.01) from noise. Then generate A–C, package with vcc prep and submit.

The PCA + ridge layer

  • Gene set: 7,283 genes measured (≥ 1 CPM) in HCT116, HEK293T and K562.
  • Programs: an uncentred PCA of every kept knockdown of six source lines (HCT116, HEK293T, K562, RPE1, HepG2, Jurkat). Each row's own knocked-down gene is set to 0. Genes a newer line did not measure (6–10% of the set) are filled iteratively from the low-rank fit (EM-PCA).
  • Scores: every source row becomes k numbers by least squares on the genes that source measured.
  • Ridge: one small ridge per program predicts one line's scores for a knockdown from the other lines' scores for the same knockdown (with "missing" flags); the destination line rotates over all lines and its own response never enters its inputs. The "corrected" variant predicts Model 2's error instead and adds it back. Penalty from a fixed grid by 5-fold cross-validation grouped by knockdown.
  • Back to genes: loadings × predicted scores. For orthonormal components this equals the least-squares regression of each gene on the component scores.

Choosing k: the noise edge and k80

  1. Compute the eigenvalues of the training table (knockdowns × genes).
  2. Build a table of pure measurement noise of the same shape, each entry drawn with its own standard error, and take its largest eigenvalue: the noise edge.
  3. Components whose eigenvalue exceeds the edge carry signal: 31 on our fits.
  4. For each signal component, its excess over the edge is its signal variance. k80 is the smallest k whose excess adds up to 80% of the total excess: 7 (5 hold 74%, 10 hold 88%, 20 hold 98.7%).

Two facts from this fit matter more than k: measurement noise is 82% of the total variance in the training tables, and 20 components explain only 19.5% of the total variance. (The notebook's prose quotes 21 components and k80 = 5 from an earlier fit; the stored fits say 31 and 7. k barely mattered, so no decision depended on it.)

How we are scored

All six metrics compare our cells with the real cells of the same line. Each is scaled from 0 (the generic baseline: the line's true average knockdown response applied to every knockdown) to 1 (a real repeat of the experiment), then the six are averaged.

MetricQuestion it asksWhat wins
pdsCan each knockdown be told apart from the others?Each predicted change pattern points at its own real change more than at any other knockdown's. Size is irrelevant.
mseIs the mean expression right?Explaining the real movement away from control; big wrong predictions are punished, squared.
nmaeFor genes that really changed, is the size right?Accurate magnitudes on true DE genes.
fidelityOf what you called, how much was right (gene and direction)?Calling about as many genes as truly changed, mostly real and in the right direction.
reachHow far down your strongest-first list do you stay ≥ 90% right?A very clean top of the list.
JaccardDoes your set of DE genes match the real set?Membership only; misses and extras both hurt.

Four of the six (nmae, fidelity, reach, Jaccard) are about differentially expressed genes, decided by a Wilcoxon test on our 400 cells against the real controls. That is why the number of genes that come out significant in our cells matters so much.

pds, worked example

  1. Start from the submission: for line A, 300 knockdowns × 400 cells × 18,533 genes of integer counts.
  2. Pseudobulk: per knockdown, add up the 400 cells' counts for each gene, scale to a fixed total, take the log. One vector of 18,533 numbers per knockdown. Do the same for the real control cells.
  3. Change from control: subtract the control vector. This is the knockdown's signed change profile. The real data get the same treatment.
  4. Drop genes: every knocked-down gene of the panel is removed from the comparison, so a knockdown can't be recognised by its own target.
  5. Compare: for our knockdown X, compute the cosine distance (one minus the cosine of the angle) between our change profile and the real change profile of every knockdown.
  6. Rank: where does the real X land among all 300? Score = 1 − rank / 299, with rank 0 = closest. Average over knockdowns, then scale (H1: generic baseline 0.488, replicate 0.992).

A toy version with 3 genes and 4 knockdowns. Our predicted change for knockdown A is (+0.8, −0.2, +0.1):

Real A is closest, so our A scores 1 − 0/3 = 1. If our prediction were ten times smaller, (+0.08, −0.02, +0.01), every distance stays the same and the score is still 1: only the direction of the change profile and the ranking matter. Had real C been closer, A would score 1 − 1/3 = 0.67. A change shared by every knockdown doesn't help any of them be told apart; dropping noisy genes does, which is why top-N calling raised pds.

mse, worked example and the "5%"

  1. Per knockdown, compare our pseudobulk with the real one (same log scale as pds, no subtraction): the mean squared difference across genes. Sampling noise from having finitely many cells is subtracted out on both sides.
  2. Per knockdown, measure how far the real knockdown moved from the real control: the mean squared difference between the two.
  3. Sum both over all knockdowns and divide: mse = Σ our error / Σ real movement. Predicting no change scores exactly 1 by design; perfect scores 0.
  4. Scale: (bar − ours) / (bar − replicate), clamped to between 0 and 1. On H1 the bar (generic baseline) is 0.953 and the replicate 0.005.

A toy version with 2 knockdowns × 3 genes (log scale). Control (5.0, 3.0, 2.0); real K1 (5.6, 2.6, 2.0); real K2 (4.6, 3.0, 2.5). Total real movement = 0.93.

The "5%": on H1 the generic baseline scores 0.953, so its error is 95.3% of the real movement: knowing each line's average response explains only about 5% of the squared movement (in the toy it explains 24%). Any submission whose ratio is below the line's bar scores above 0, so the bar looks low. We still miss it: our arms score 1.39 to 6.89 on H1, worse than predicting nothing. Big wrong predictions cost more, squared, than small right ones gain.

A puzzle in our numbers: predicting no change should score exactly 1, but our "no change" cells score 1.14 (H1, twice). See fixing mse.

The shared response

Every knockdown in a screen shares part of its effect, whichever gene was knocked down: CRISPR machinery and guide effects, slowed growth or stress, cell-cycle shifts, the screen's own culture and batch. It is measured as the average change over all knockdowns of a line. On H1 roughly half of each knockdown's significantly changed genes belong to it.

It matters for three reasons:

  • The leaderboard's 0 already contains it. The generic baseline is exactly this average, from the line's own real data.
  • It is lab-specific. Predicting one line's shared response from the others: HCT116 r = 0.53 and HEK293T r = 0.51 (both Orion, so possibly a lab effect), K562 0.23, H1 0.01. H1 agrees with none of our sources.
  • Our model largely misses it. Model 2 averages each source's total effect, so it carries the sources' shared responses (other labs) rather than the target line's.

Why H1 can mislead: it is one line, embryonic stem cells with an unusually strong shared response that agrees with no other line; we score 75 knockdowns, so differences under about ±0.01 are noise; and we choose settings on it, which flatters the winner. In its favour: it is the only line from the same lab and protocol as A–F with raw knockdown cells.

Why swapping is hard: a validation line needs raw single cells for its knockdowns (Orion and the T cells are pseudobulk only), it must be removed from the model's training sources, and each line costs about 4 hours to build its scoring reference. K562 (62 GB, in our bucket), RPE1, HepG2 and Jurkat (5–9 GB each, public) qualify; none is from Arc.

Recommendation: keep H1 as a screen, add one second line (Jurkat or HepG2 are the cheapest), trust only decisions both lines agree on by more than the noise, and spend leaderboard submissions on changes that trade one metric for another, as v4 did.

The mathematician's suggestions

Written on the premise that rank ~750 came from the PCA model. It came from Model 2 with top-N calling; the PCA variants tied or lost. With that correction:

SuggestionDid we do it?Merit for this task
1. Train/test split to choose the number of componentsYes: 5-fold CV grouped by knockdown, a noise-edge rule, and a whole held-out line (H1). k barely mattered (CV error 0.0417 at k = 5, 0.0414 at k = 20).Sound; the split that matters is by cell line, since we predict known knockdowns in an unseen line. Holding out knockdowns answers an easier question.
2. Down-weight later components continuously instead of cutting at nPartly: each program's ridge shrinks its scores.Right in principle (ridge in gene space is exactly smooth shrinkage of components). Small expected gain: the training tables are 82% noise and transfer, not k, is the bottleneck.
3. If genes A and B co-respond, knocking down A or B may act alikeNo: each knockdown is predicted only from its own measurements in other lines.New and worth testing. Most useful for the 74 of 300 knockdowns with 0–1 source lines (18 currently get only their own drop). Testable on H1 by hiding sources.
4. Cluster knockdowns, fit per cluster, ensemble with the global modelNo.Worth testing, as partial pooling: pull each knockdown's noisy estimate toward its cluster average. With 82% noise this attacks exactly what hurts mse.
5. A fitted, regularised per-gene regression to map components back to genesWe use loadings × scores.Equal to the per-gene least-squares fit for orthonormal components on the same data; a separately fitted version mostly adds overfitting. Low priority.

What is hard to see from outside: the score is relative to a baseline that already knows each line's average response; four of six metrics are about which genes come out significant; and the generator is not the limit (oracle 0.85). Suggestions 3 and 4 reduce noise in the effects, which is what the profile metrics need.

Three sources of variance

SourceHow we model itStatus
1. True effect differences between lines (biology)τ² in Model 2 down-weights genes where lines disagree, but it mixes this with source 3.Partly modelled; cannot separate biology from lab.

3. Technical and lab differences between datasetsWithin-dataset noise is in each SE; systematic offsets (a lab's shared response) are not modelled and end up in τ².gap The shared response is lab-specific. A source-specific offset (remove each source's shared response before averaging, add the target line's back) addresses this and mse together.

Diagnosis. The generator can reach mse 0.77 with true effects, so the cells are not the limit. Every effect set we have predicted is further from the truth than no change, and shrinking can only bring us back toward no change (1.14), never under the bar (0.953). Halving even the true effects made mse worse (0.63 vs 0.77 scaled).

Leads, cheapest first:

  1. Our "no change" costs 0.14 too much. By design it should score 1.00. One likely cause: every knockdown reuses the same 400 control cells, so that sample's chance deviation from the line's true control mean is identical in all 300 predictions. The scorer subtracts sampling noise only up to the spread between our predictions, so a deviation common to all of them is never removed. The generator already has an unused match_pool option that corrects each knockdown for its pool's deviation, and fresh draws per knockdown are another fix. Test: a no-change arm with each fix on H1, about 30 minutes per arm. Expected: no change near 1.00.
  2. Add the shared response. Without our own estimate of the line's average response we cannot beat a baseline that has the true one. For A–F it must come from elsewhere; H1's (same lab and protocol) is the best candidate. Only the leaderboard can test it, because on H1 it would be H1's own answer.
  3. Make the knockdown-specific part help instead of hurt. Shrink each gene by how reliable its prediction is rather than by one global factor; pool similar knockdowns (suggestions 3 and 4); remove each source's own shared response before averaging.
  4. A cheap feasibility check: in effect space (no cell generation, minutes), compute H1's mse for "H1's true average response + x × our specific Δ" over a grid of x. If every x > 0 is worse than x = 0, the specific part must improve before the shared response can pay.

A tension to keep in view: the DE metrics reward changes large enough to be detected in 400 cells; mse punishes large changes that are wrong. mse is computed on mean expression, the DE metrics on cell-level tests.
